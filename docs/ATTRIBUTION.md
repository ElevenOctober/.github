# Attribution

Finding a meaningful problem is a contribution. Preserve credit for the person or community that identified it, as well as the people and materials that made the engineering work possible.

## Credit

Project records and articles should consider:

- The original problem reporter and affected community
- Upstream open-source projects and maintainers
- Datasets, libraries, papers, datasheets, standards, and other references
- Contributors, reviewers, designers, and testers

Credit should describe what each source or contributor contributed. Do not imply endorsement or authorship that was not agreed.

## Requester privacy

Choose public attribution deliberately. Ask before naming a person or organization in an article, README, presentation, or release. A public issue already displays its GitHub author; the issue form explains that its attribution preference cannot hide this platform display.

If a requester asks not to be named, use an anonymous or community-level credit where useful. Do not publish private email addresses, phone numbers, addresses, credentials, medical records, sensitive personal data, or confidential material. Keep private verification details only when necessary and with restricted access.

Do not remove the origin of a problem merely because the solution is technically interesting. If attribution must be withheld for safety, privacy, or permission reasons, say that the source was withheld without revealing identifying details.

## Provenance record

Use the block in the [problem lifecycle](PROBLEM_LIFECYCLE.md) for every accepted problem. Cite original URLs and dates where available. If a source is not public, record how it was verified without publishing private evidence.

## Reuse and licenses

Check the license and terms of external code, hardware, images, data, and writing before reuse. Follow the applicable license and retain required notices. New project repositories must state their own license; this community repository’s license does not automatically cover them.
